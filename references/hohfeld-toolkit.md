# Hohfeld Toolkit — disambiguating relation-words

Source: Wesley N. Hohfeld, *Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning*, 23 Yale L.J. 16 (1913).

Use this when a classificatory move turns on a **relation-word** — *right, duty, property, power, interest, title, possession, liberty, immunity* — and you suspect the word is doing more than one job. Hohfeld's apparatus is not a database schema to fill in on every case; it is a **diagnostic for equivocation**. Reach for it when an argument's validity seems to depend on a legal word quietly shifting sense.

## 1. The problem Hohfeld is solving

Legal reasoning goes wrong, Hohfeld argues, because a handful of words are "chameleon-hued": the same term is used, often within one argument, for legally different things. The chief offender is **"right,"** which gets used loosely to cover what are really four distinct relations. "Property," "power," "liberty," and "interest" are nearly as bad. The remedy is not a new vocabulary for its own sake but a way to force each legal assertion into a definite relation between definite parties over a definite matter — so that a hidden shift of sense cannot pass for a valid inference.

Two prior confusions set the stage in the article and are worth carrying as independent checks:
- **Legal vs. physical/mental facts.** "Transfer," "power," "possession" all borrow from the physical world; a legal *power* is not a physical capacity, and treating them as the same is a category error.
- **Operative vs. evidential facts.** The facts that *change* legal relations (operative/dispositive) are not the facts that merely *prove* them (evidential). Arguments that blur these misstate what a case actually turned on.

  This line anchors §1 above: the physical/mental facts (an offer spoken, a watch handed over, land occupied) *call the relation into being*, but are not the relation. Every equivocation the toolkit catches is a version of confusing the two.

- On why it is worth the trouble, closing the discussion (23 Yale L.J., at 58–59): reduce the tangle of legal quantities to their "lowest common denominators" — the eight conceptions — and superficially different problems become comparable, revealing "fundamental unity and harmony in the law."

## 2. The eight conceptions: opposites and correlatives

Hohfeld isolates four pairs. Read the tables as: within a pair of **correlatives**, the two names describe *one and the same relation from the two parties' sides*; a pair of **opposites** are the presence and the absence of the same position in one party.

**Correlatives** (same relation, two sides):

| One party has… | …the other party has |
|---|---|
| a **right** (claim) | a **duty** |
| a **privilege** (liberty) | a **no-right** |
| a **power** | a **liability** |
| an **immunity** | a **disability** |

**Opposites** (presence vs. absence in one party):

| Position | Its opposite |
|---|---|
| **right** | **no-right** |
| **privilege** | **duty** |
| **power** | **disability** |
| **immunity** | **liability** |

Working definitions:
- **Right (claim-right):** X can require Y to act or forbear; the correlative is Y's **duty**. This is "right" in the strict sense. *Test: if the correlative in the other party is not a duty, you are not dealing with a right in the strict sense.*
- **Privilege (liberty):** X is free to act (or not) — X has *no duty* to the contrary; the correlative is Y's **no-right** (Y has no claim that X not do it). A privilege is the *negation of a duty* with opposite content. Crucially, a privilege does **not** by itself entail any duty in others not to interfere.
- **Power:** X can, by some act, *change* legal relations (create, extinguish, transfer them); the correlative is Y's **liability** (Y is subject to having the relation changed). An offer confers on the offeree a power; the offeror is under a liability. Agency, conveyance, abandonment, waiver, election — all are powers.
- **Immunity:** X is *not subject* to having a given legal relation changed by Y; the correlative is Y's **disability** (Y lacks the power). A tax exemption is an immunity, not a "right."

## 3. The relational drill

For any asserted legal position, force these four questions. If the argument cannot answer them consistently, it is trading on ambiguity:

1. **Which of the four** is it — right, privilege, power, or immunity? (Fix it by naming the correlative: duty? no-right? liability? disability?)
2. **Who holds it** (the holder)?
3. **Against whom** does it run — a specific party, a class, an official, or the world at large? (In-rem-style positions run against a large class; say which.)
4. **Over what** act, forbearance, thing, event, or *legal change*?

Then check whether the argument keeps the answer *fixed* as it moves to its conclusion. Most Hohfeldian errors are a silent switch between two of the four across steps 1→conclusion.

## 4. The confusions to catch (article's own examples)

- **"Property" = thing vs. legal interest.** The word slides between the physical object and the aggregate of legal relations over it. Blackstone's "corporeal / incorporeal hereditaments" enacts the confusion: *all* legal interests are "incorporeal" (they are relations, not things), so a contrast between corporeal and incorporeal property is spurious. Watch for an argument that infers a legal consequence from "property" while meaning the thing.
- **"Right" doing four jobs.** Courts use "right" to mean, variously, a claim, a privilege, a power, or an immunity (Hohfeld cites *People v. Dikeman*, *U.S. v. Patrick*, *Lonas v. State*). Each carries a different correlative and a different consequence; an argument that uses "right" without fixing which one is not yet an argument.
- **Privilege confused with right — the salad example.** X's *privilege* to eat the salad (others have a no-right) is a different relation from X's *right* that others not seize it (others have a duty). One can exist without the other: A, B, C might license X to eat ("you have our leave") without agreeing not to interfere — X has the privilege but no right against interference. Arguments that infer a right-against-interference from a bare privilege are invalid.
- **"Liberty" transmuted into "right" mid-argument — *Quinn v. Leathem*.** Lord Lindley's passage begins with the plaintiff's *liberty* to pursue his trade and, "under the seductive influence" of the word, slides to a *right*, and then assumes a correlative *duty in everyone not to interfere*. The only correlative a liberty logically implies is a *no-right* in others; whether there is also a duty not to interfere is a separate question of justice and policy, not something the word "liberty" delivers.
- **Two "rights" that are really a right and a privilege — *Mogul Steamship v. McGregor*.** The "apparent conflict" between the plaintiffs' "right" to trade and the defendants' "right" to trade dissolves once you see that one is a claim-**right** and the other a **privilege**: a right and a privilege of opposite content do not actually conflict.
- **Power vs. physical power; the offer/option.** A contractual offer creates in the offeree a **power** (to bind by acceptance) and in the offeror a **liability**; "the offer remains open" describes the persistence of that power-liability relation, not a physical or mental state. Options, agency authority, escrow (the grantee's power to divest title by performing conditions), and a right-of-entry on condition broken are all powers, routinely mislabeled "rights."
- **Immunity vs. right — tax exemptions.** In the exemption cases (*Phoenix Ins. Co. v. Tennessee*, *Morgan v. Louisiana*), what a charter confers is an **immunity** (the state is under a **disability** to tax), not a "right." Calling it a right obscures both its correlative and its alienability.

## 5. Why it controls outcomes

Hohfeld's practical thesis: getting the relation right is not pedantry — it can decide cases. Whether an interest is a right or a privilege determines whether interference is a wrong; whether a position is a power or a right determines what act changes it and who is exposed; whether it is an immunity or a right determines whether the state can reach it and whether it passes on transfer. The salad, *Quinn v. Leathem*, and the exemption cases each turn on the distinction, not on the loose word.

## 6. How to use it here — and when not to

**Use it** when a classificatory/relational move is load-bearing and a relation-word looks like it is shifting sense — especially inferences of the form "X has a right/property/power, *therefore* Y must/cannot…". Run the relational drill on the premise and the conclusion and see whether the relation stayed fixed.

**Do not** build a full eight-cell relation map on every input. Most arguments do not turn on a relational equivocation, and a table where none is needed is noise. When a full, structured relation map *is* wanted for a specific judgment (archival digestion), that machinery lives in `precedent-extraction.md`. Here, the tool is the *drill and the catches*, applied surgically to the joint that needs it.
