# Precedent Method — how precedent actually constrains

Source: Neil Duxbury, *The Nature and Authority of Precedent* (Cambridge University Press, 2008). Synthesis in my own words; no text reproduced.

Use this whenever a **precedent-application move** is load-bearing — an argument that a decided case governs, supports, or forecloses the present question. The core lesson is that precedent-following is real but *defeasible and contestable*: it is not mechanical binding, and the steps an argument needs (ratio, material similarity, level of generality, availability of a distinction) are exactly the steps most likely to be assumed rather than earned.

## 1. The central picture: presumptions, not a rule-machine

Duxbury's organizing claim is that **no single principle or theory explains the authority of precedent**. Instead there is a cluster of arguments, each raising a *rebuttable presumption* in favour of following an earlier decision. The doctrine's principal requirement is that courts respect earlier decisions on **materially identical facts** — but the doctrine *also* requires courts to **depart** from such decisions when following them would perpetuate legal error or injustice.

The consequence for analysis: precedents do **not** "bind" in the classical-positivist sense (a rule that dictates the result regardless of its merits). Were they to bind like that, they would be ill-suited to common-law decision-making, which develops precisely by testing, narrowing, and occasionally overturning past decisions. So when an argument says "we are bound by X," treat that as a *presumption to be examined*, not a conclusion: bound to what proposition, at what level, on what similarity, and is this a case where departure is warranted?

## 2. Ratio decidendi vs. obiter dictum

The distinction is genuine and has endured, but it is **not easily made, and there is no reliable mechanical test**. Carry these points:

- **Ratio ≠ the reasoning as such.** "Ratio decidendi" means the reason *for* the decision — the proposition, tied to the material facts and necessary to the disposition, on which the result rests. The judge's reasoning may be integral to it, but not all of a court's reasons, however general, are part of the ratio. Some reasoning within a judgment is itself obiter.
- **Obiter = "said by the way."** Dicta come in forms: unnecessary to the outcome, unconnected to the material facts, or addressed to a point neither party argued. They may have been formulated with less care than a passage that carried the decision.
- **The old "bye-matter" test and its limits.** A rough test (traceable to Fulbeck c.1600 and Vaughan C.J. in *Bole v. Horton*, 1673): a point is mere dictum if it could be removed, or reversed, without altering the decision. Useful as a first cut — but **not reliable**, and it is harsh to treat a peripheral opinion as no opinion at all.
- **Dicta are not worthless.** Weighty dicta — a higher court's considered statement, an authoritative judge's formulation of principle — can carry heavy persuasive force, sometimes followed in preference to the ratio of a lower court (Duxbury's examples include courts deferring to considered dicta of eminent judges). And precedents do work by **analogy**, not only by binding ratio; much legal argument uses cases analogically, where the binding/non-binding line is not the point.
- **Why the concept confounds.** Ratio and dicta blur; dissents can partially agree and thereby touch the ratio; a majority judge may add reasons without making them ratio; later courts may promote a past dictum to operative force. Llewellyn catalogued dozens of legitimate techniques for handling precedent; there is no "single answer."

**Diagnostic upshot:** never accept a clean ratio/dictum split as a given. If an argument rests on "the court *held* P," ask whether P is tied to the material facts and necessary to the disposition, or whether P is a general statement the court made along the way. A weighty dictum can still be argued from — but as persuasive authority, not as binding ratio.

## 3. Material similarity is a judgment

Precedent binds on materially *identical* facts — which throws all the weight onto whether *this* case's facts are materially like the cited case's. **That is a judgment about which differences matter, not a lookup.** Every case differs from every other in countless ways; the analytic work is deciding which differences are material (defeat the analogy) and which are immaterial (leave it intact). An argument that asserts "indistinguishable" or "on all fours" without defending *which* differences it is treating as immaterial has skipped the actual reasoning. This is almost always the true battleground of a precedent dispute.

---

## 4. Level of generality

The same decision can be read narrowly (a holding tightly bound to its facts) or broadly (a wide principle). A precedent is stretched to cover what it never decided by reading it at too high a level of generality. When an argument leans on a case, pin down: at what level is the holding being taken, and does the case — read against its material facts and disposition — actually support *that* level, or only a narrower one?

---

## 5. The repertoire of moves

A later court is not limited to "follow or ignore." The live options, each a distinct analytic move:

- **Follow** — apply the precedent's ratio to materially similar facts.
- **Distinguish** — respect the precedent yet decline to apply it, by identifying a *material* factual difference between the cases. Distinguishing is the ordinary, legitimate way the common law narrows a rule from case to case; an available distinction that an argument ignores is a weakness in that argument.
- **Extend** — apply the precedent's principle to a new fact-setting by analogy (watch the level-of-generality move).
- **Limit / narrow** — read the precedent more tightly than before, confining it to its facts.
- **Depart** — decline to follow where following would perpetuate error or injustice (available especially to a court not strictly bound, or a court with power over its own prior decisions).
- **Overrule** — a court competent to do so declares the earlier decision no longer law. Overruling raises its own puzzles (e.g., a court's power to overrule its *own* prior decisions, and the status of a self-imposed practice of doing so). Do **not** infer overruling from mere non-application; overruling is an express, hierarchically competent act.

Match the argument's actual move to the right label, and check that the court in question had the authority for it (hierarchy, jurisdiction, and — for overruling one's own decisions — the constitutional competence to do so).

## 6. Why courts follow precedent (and why it matters to the analysis)

Duxbury sorts the justifications into two families. Knowing which one an argument is leaning on tells you how to test it:

- **Consequentialist** — certainty and predictability, protection of reliance, efficiency (not re-deciding settled questions), and constraint on judicial arbitrariness. These are *policy* reasons; test them as policy moves (are the consequences real? do they hold here?).
- **Deontological** — fairness and formal justice: like cases should be treated alike; it is unfair to decide this litigant's case differently from a materially identical earlier one. This is a *normative* reason grounded in equality, and it is the strongest ground for the presumption — but it presupposes material identity, which loops back to §3.

Because the authority of precedent rests on presumptions of these kinds rather than on a binding rule, the presumption is **rebuttable**: it can be outweighed where following would perpetuate error or injustice, or where the cases are not materially alike after all.

---

## 7. Diagnostic checklist

For any precedent-application move:

1. **Holds what?** Is the claimed proposition the *ratio* (tied to material facts, necessary to disposition) or a *dictum*? If dictum, how weighty — argue it as persuasive, not binding.
2. **Materially similar?** Which differences between this case and the cited one is the argument treating as immaterial, and is that defensible?
3. **What level?** Is the holding being read at a level of generality the case actually supports?
4. **Distinction available?** Is there a material difference that would let a court distinguish — and is the argument ignoring it?
5. **Which move, with what authority?** Follow / distinguish / extend / limit / depart / overrule — labelled correctly, and within the deciding court's competence and jurisdiction?
6. **Presumption or conclusion?** Is "we are bound" being treated as a rebuttable presumption (correct) or as a rule-machine result (a mistake about how precedent works)?
7. **Departure warranted?** If following would perpetuate a clear error or injustice, is this a case where the doctrine itself licenses departure?
